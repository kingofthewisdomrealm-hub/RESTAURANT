Six Calls Standing Orders

Restaurant build‑out, commercial tenant space. Six questions come up on every one of these jobs that no code book answers. This document answers them once, for everybody, so nobody on a Covenant job has to make the call alone at seven in the morning.

The rule behind all six

Every one of these is a question somebody else has to answer — and every one of them gets cheaper the earlier you ask it.

Four are a phone call. Two are arithmetic you can do at a desk. None of them is a field decision. Ask, get the answer in writing, and write it on the permit card where the next man can read it.

If you find yourself deciding one of these six on site, you are already behind. Stop and call the office.

01SOP

The seat count

Standing order

We do not submit a seat count until the plan reviewer has told us in writing what that count triggers — and we price what it triggers into the bid before the owner signs.

Owner
Project Manager
When
Before drawings go in for permit
Ends up
On the cover sheet

Do this

  1. Measure the net dining area and the net kitchen area off the lease exhibit.
  2. Divide dining by 15 sq ft per person. Divide kitchen by 200. Add them. That is your occupant load.
  3. If the total is 50 or more, the space is Group A‑2 — assembly. That buys two exits, doors that swing out, and panic hardware on the exit doors. Price all three now.
  4. Email the plan reviewer the number and ask him to confirm the classification and what it triggers.
  5. Order the panic hardware the day he confirms. Panic devices run about six weeks.
  6. If the owner later wants more seats, this SOP runs again from step 1. Seats are not a finish selection.

Say this — to the plan reviewer

“I have a retail bay going to a restaurant. My net dining is about 1,150 square feet and the kitchen is about 1,000. That puts me at roughly 81 occupants at 15 and 200. I read that as Group A‑2. Can you confirm the classification, and confirm whether I’m into two exits and panic hardware at that count? I’d like it in an email so I can order hardware.”

Record it: the reviewer’s reply in the project folder, the occupant load on the cover sheet, and the hardware on the buyout log the same day.

Skip it

The fire marshal does the arithmetic for you at his final. That is about six weeks of a finished building you cannot get a CO on, while a panic device ships.

Source: FBC / IBC Table 1004.5 — assembly unconcentrated (tables and chairs) 15 net, commercial kitchens 200 gross. IBC 303.1.1 — assembly classification at 50. IBC 1010.2.9 — panic hardware for Group A at occupant load 50 or more. ✓ Robust.

02SOP

The capacity charge

Standing order

No client of ours signs a restaurant lease until Indian River County Utilities has given us the ERU count and the charge, in writing. If the lease is already signed, we put the number in front of them the same day we are engaged.

Owner
Estimator, PM copied
When
Before the lease is executed
Ends up
Its own bid line

Do this

  1. Call Utilities with the address, the proposed use, the seat count and the fixture count.
  2. Ask for two numbers: how many ERUs they will assign this restaurant, and the charge per ERU. Ask for both in writing.
  3. Multiply. Then add the service connection fees and the meter install.
  4. Put that total in the bid as its own line. It does not go in allowances and it does not go in general conditions.
  5. If the lease is already signed, send it to the client in writing today. It is still their money — but it is no longer a surprise, and that is the whole difference.

Say this — to Indian River County Utilities

“I’m pricing a change of use at [address] — retail going to a restaurant, about 80 seats. I need two things in writing before my client signs a lease. First, how many ERUs will you assign that use. Second, your current water and sewer impact fee per ERU, plus the service connection and meter fees. Can you email that to me?”

Record it: the utility’s written reply in the project folder, and the line item in the estimate with the reply attached.

Skip it

The published fee is $4,096 a connection — $1,300 water, $2,796 sewer. A shoe store is about one connection. A restaurant is not. Six more is $24,576, and it lands the day you go to set the meter, with no budget line behind it.

Source: Indian River County Department of Utility Services, water and sewer rate schedule — impact fees effective 1 October 2022: water $1,300.00/ERU, sewer $2,796.00/ERU; service connection water $2,785, sewer $2,895. ~ Range — confirm the current schedule; these figures are three years old, and the ERU count for a non‑residential conversion is not published anywhere. That is why this is a phone call.

03SOP

The interceptor size

Standing order

We size the grease interceptor from a counted fixture unit schedule, confirm it with the utility, and set it outside — before anybody breaks ground. Never from memory. Never under a sink without the authority’s written approval.

Owner
Plumbing sub, verified by the PM
When
After underground passes, before the excavator
Ends up
Project folder

Do this

  1. Have the plumber produce a written drainage fixture unit count for everything on the grease line — three‑compartment sink, prep sink, mop sink, dishmachine, every floor sink under the cook line.
  2. Read the gravity table: 8 DFU takes 500 gal · 21 takes 750 · 35 takes 1,000 · 90 takes 1,250.
  3. Call the utility with your count and the size it lands on. Ask them to confirm, and ask whether their ordinance amends it. Theirs is the number that gets inspected.
  4. Set it outside: two chambers, sample point downstream, a traffic‑rated lid anywhere a truck can drive, and clear space for a pump hose to reach.
  5. An under‑sink unit goes in only with written approval from the authority, and only if the flow works — those run about 20 to 50 gallons a minute and a full kitchen walks straight past that.

Say this — to the utility

“I’ve got a full‑service kitchen at [address]. My plumber counted [n] drainage fixture units on the grease line, which puts me on the gravity table at [size] gallons. Two questions: does your FOG ordinance amend that number, and do you want it at that size or larger? And can you confirm your lid and sample port requirements for a traffic area?”

Record it: the DFU schedule and the utility’s confirmation, both in the project folder, before the excavator mobilises.

Skip it

Guess low and the utility rejects it. That is about three weeks and $9,000 — you dig up a tank you already paid to set, through asphalt you already paid to patch.

Source: FBC Plumbing / PDI gravity interceptor sizing by drainage fixture units; gravity units installed outside the building unless the authority approves otherwise; hydromechanical units sized by flow, commonly 20–50 gpm. ✓ Robust as a method — the local FOG ordinance still controls the final number.

04SOP

Who accepts it

Standing order

We establish the acceptance sequence for the interceptor and the backflow at permit pickup, in two phone calls — and we write both answers on the permit card. Not the week we need the meter.

Owner
Project Manager
When
The week the permit is issued
Ends up
On the permit card, in ink

Do this

  1. Call Indian River County Utilities. Ask who accepts the interceptor and who files the backflow certificate.
  2. Call the plumbing inspector. Ask whether it is its own inspection or rolls into site plumbing, and what has to be complete before he will come out.
  3. Write both answers on the permit card, in ink, where the foreman actually reads it.
  4. Book the licensed backflow tester before the device is installed, not after. The certificate is what releases the meter, and the tester is not always available this week.

Say this — two calls, same morning

To Utilities: “I’m setting a grease interceptor and an RPZ backflow at [address]. Who on your side accepts the interceptor, and where does the backflow certificate get filed? What do you need from me before you’ll release the meter?” To the plumbing inspector: “Is the interceptor and backflow its own inspection on a commercial job, or does it roll into site plumbing? And what do you need complete before you come out?”

→ Permit card

Skip it

Assume the building inspector covers it and he does not release your water meter — the utility does. No meter, no water, no finish inspections. That is about a month of sitting on a finished‑out building, dry, paying rent.

Source: none published — county, state or code. This is the only step in this document with no document behind it, which is exactly why it is a phone call and not a lookup. Confirm it on the first job and write the answer here. ◈ Model.

05SOP

The sprinkler question

Standing order

We check all three sprinkler triggers on every commercial job and get the plan reviewer’s written confirmation before demolition starts. The building’s history is not evidence.

Owner
Project Manager
When
With the permit application, before demo
Ends up
On the cover sheet

Do this

  1. Calculate the fire area in square feet.
  2. Calculate the occupant load — SOP 01 gives you the number.
  3. Confirm whether the space sits on the level of exit discharge.
  4. A Group A‑2 fire area needs sprinklers if any one of these is true: it exceeds 5,000 sq ft · the occupant load is 100 or more · it is on a floor other than the level of exit discharge.
  5. Send all three numbers to the plan reviewer and ask him to confirm, in writing, which — if any — applies.
  6. If one trips, stop and re‑price. This is no longer a build‑out and it should not be bid as one.

Say this — to the plan reviewer

“Group A‑2 tenant space at [address]. Fire area is [n] square feet, occupant load is [n], and it’s on the level of exit discharge. By 903.2.1.2 I read that as clearing all three triggers — no sprinkler required. Can you confirm that in writing before I start demo?”

Record it: all three numbers and the reviewer’s reply on the cover sheet, so the fire marshal can see the arithmetic at his final without taking anybody’s word for it.

Skip it

Finding out at the fire marshal final that the job needed a sprinkler system is not a change order. It is a different project, on a lease that has been running rent the whole time.

Source: FBC Building 8th Ed. / IBC 903.2.1.2 — Group A‑2: fire area exceeding 5,000 sq ft, occupant load of 100 or more, or a fire area located on a floor other than the level of exit discharge. ✓ Robust.

06SOP

The call order

Standing order

We get the commercial inspection sequence from the building department, at the counter, and write it on the permit card. We do not use the published priority sheet. It is residential.

Owner
PM, then the superintendent
When
Permit pickup
Ends up
On the permit card, in ink

Do this

  1. At the counter, ask for the inspection sequence for a commercial interior alteration — which inspection, in what order, and which ones are prerequisites for which.
  2. Write it on the permit card in the order it will actually happen on this job.
  3. Brief the superintendent before the first trade mobilises. He is the one booking them.
  4. Do not hand anybody the 2019 BRCOM sheet. It is a house. If it is already in the job binder, take it out.

Say this — at the permit counter

“This is a commercial interior alteration — restaurant build‑out in a leased bay. What’s your inspection sequence for this permit type? Which ones are priority 1, and what has to be resulted and passed before I can book the next tier? I want to write it on the card so my super books them in the right order.”

→ Permit card

Skip it

The published sheet is a house — footing, tie beam, truss bracing, roof sheathing, strapping, dry‑in. And the scheduling system enforces priority: it will not let you book a priority 2 until every priority 1 is resulted and passed. Your calls do not get argued with. They get declined — about two weeks of them, with trades standing by.

Source: Indian River County / City of Vero Beach Building Division, 2019_BRCOM_Scheduling — headed “BRCOM RESIDENTIAL COMBINATION NEW CONSTRUCTION”, and it states the system will not allow scheduling a priority 2 until all priority 1s are resulted and passed. No commercial equivalent found published. ✓ Robust.
